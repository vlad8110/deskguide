> From a Solutions Engineer perspective, DeskGuide must isolate what can change from what needs to remain dependable. The external AI provider is an uncertain dependency: it can time out, exceed quota, or generate a plausible response that lacks supporting evidence. A dedicated AI adapter, retrieval component and evidence-validation boundary therefore make more sense than allowing model-generated text to control customer permissions, customer-database lookups or ticket submission. The application enforces authentication, authorization and input validation. Retrieval owns approved passages and stable source IDs, while escalation owns a durable, single-save request with safe retry semantics. An AI failure should not automatically disable the customer's ability to request help, although escalation capacity and data-store availability also require limits. Designing for 10,000 concurrent customer sessions introduces production decisions around shared state, durable concurrent storage, provider quotas and overload protection. Our original local Flask/SQLite prototype cannot be cited as evidence of that capacity. The system's logical modules should remain independently testable with mocked external dependencies so junior developers can verify behavior without operating a distributed production environment. Parnas (1972) argues that modules should conceal design decisions likely to change. Provider and persistence implementations are exactly such decisions for DeskGuide. A modular application may simplify student development and maintenance, but Vlad must evaluate whether its deployment and storage strategy can meet the stated production workload and document its operational tradeoffs.
